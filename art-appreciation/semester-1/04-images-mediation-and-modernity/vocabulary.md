# Vocabulary: Images, Mediation, and Modernity

*From Aura to the Feed*

**1. Visuality**
The historically specific way of seeing and being seen in a given time and culture. It includes not only what we look at, but how we are taught to look.

**2. Mediation**
All the layers that stand between us and the world when we see something, for example cameras, screens, editing, museums, or social media platforms.

**3. Ways of seeing**
John Berger's idea that images are never neutral. Every image reflects a particular point of view and invites a particular kind of viewer.

**4. Aura**
Walter Benjamin's term for the unique presence or "special glow" of an original artwork, tied to its history, location, and singularity.

**5. Mechanical reproduction**
The process of making many copies of an image using machines such as the printing press, camera, or film projector, which weakens the aura of the original.

**6. Cult value**
For Benjamin, the value an artwork has when it is tied to ritual, worship, or private devotion rather than to public display.

**7. Exhibition value**
The value an artwork gains when it is made to be shown to many viewers, for example in museums, galleries, books, and online.

**8. Technical image**
Vilém Flusser's term for images produced by devices such as cameras, scanners, or phones. These images are shaped by programs and settings, not only by the artist's hand.

**9. Apparatus**
A programmed device or system, such as a camera, phone, or app, that controls what kinds of images can be made and how they appear.

**10. Spectatorship**
The position and experience of the viewer. It includes where the viewer is placed, what they are allowed to see, and how they are expected to respond.

**11. Serial image**
A sequence of related images shown together, for example Muybridge's frames of a running horse or Warhol's repeated Marilyn faces.

**12. Fragmented perception**
A way of seeing in which reality appears as separate pieces or moments, such as frames in film or quick cuts in editing, rather than as one continuous flow.

**13. Limit case**
An artwork that pushes a visual idea to an extreme or "zero point," for example a nearly blank painting, in order to test what still counts as art.

**14. Perceptual equivalence**
Arthur Danto's idea that a work of art and an ordinary object can be visually indistinguishable, so that the difference between them depends on context and theory, not on appearance.

**15. Artworld**
The network of institutions, people, and ideas that define what counts as art, including museums, critics, artists, historians, markets, and theories.

**16. "End of Art" (Danto)**
Danto's claim that there is no longer a single story of artistic progress through styles. After a certain point, many different kinds of art can exist at the same time, and art is defined by concepts rather than by one shared style.

**17. Celebrity image**
An image of a famous person that circulates widely and develops its own power, sometimes overshadowing the real life of the person it represents.

**18. Simulacrum**
An image or sign that no longer has a clear or stable original behind it. It is a copy that refers mainly to other copies or to itself.

**19. Hyperreality**
Jean Baudrillard's term for a condition in which images and simulations are experienced as more intense or convincing than any underlying reality.

**20. Relational artwork**
An artwork that uses social interactions, gatherings, or shared activities as its main material, so that the relationships between people are part of the work.

**21. Paratheatre**
Jerzy Grotowski's attempt to move beyond traditional theatre by using exercises, rituals, and encounters that blur the line between performance and everyday life.

**22. Poor image**
Hito Steyerl's term for low resolution, compressed, or damaged images that circulate quickly online. These images lose visual quality but may gain political or cultural power through their speed and reach.

**23. Permanent collection**
The group of artworks that a museum or institution owns and keeps as part of its long term identity, usually seen as stable and worth preserving.

**24. Archive**
A structured store of documents or images, organised to be kept, classified, and accessed over time. Archives give images a long term home and official status.

**25. Feed**
A constantly updating stream of content on a digital platform, such as Instagram or TikTok, where images appear briefly and are quickly replaced.

**26. Image circulation**
The paths by which images move through society, for example through printing, broadcasting, file sharing, reposting, and algorithmic recommendation.

**27. Image regime**
A term for the overall system that controls how images are produced, distributed, and seen at a certain historical moment.

**28. Index (indexical image)**
An image that has a direct physical connection to what it shows, such as a photograph created by light hitting film or a sensor.

**29. Icon (iconic image)**
An image that resembles what it shows or becomes widely recognised as a symbol of a person, event, or idea, for example the Mona Lisa or the Great Wave.

**30. Institutional critique**
Art that examines or challenges the power of art institutions, such as museums and galleries, and the ways they decide what is shown, collected, and remembered.
