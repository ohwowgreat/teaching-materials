# Vocabulary: Food and Ethics

| Term | Meaning and Example in Art or Reading |
|------|---------------------------------------|
| **Ethics** | Principles of right and wrong behavior. In this unit, ethics means acting responsibly toward others through what we eat or share. Berger calls eating "a moral act." |
| **Representation** | The way an artist shows or interprets the world. A still life's objects and arrangement reveal values about class, faith, and desire. |
| **Labor** | Work done by hand or mind. Vermeer's *Milkmaid* and Weems's *Kitchen Table Series* show labor as dignity and care. |
| **Care** | Attention and responsibility toward others. Pouring milk or setting a table becomes an act of care and respect. |
| **Communion** | A shared act of eating or spiritual unity. In *The Last Supper* it symbolizes belief and togetherness. |
| **Appetite** | Desire for food or pleasure. In still lifes by Heda or Peeters, appetite expresses both delight and warning. |
| **Abundance** | Great plenty. Abundance can show joy or greed. Bruegel and Steen's feasts contrast with Hogarth's moral decay. |
| **Restraint** | Control or simplicity. Vermeer's quiet scene and Zurbarán's still life show humility through calm order. |
| **Commodity** | An object bought, sold, or traded. Sugar, citrus, and glass in Dutch still lifes represent colonial trade and value. |
| **Consumption** | The act of using or devouring. Berger writes that "the eater devours not only food but the world itself." |
| **Pleasure** | Enjoyment or satisfaction. Pleasure can reveal gratitude or excess. The question is when pleasure becomes ethical. |
| **Sharing** | Giving part of something to others. For Berger, "sharing bread was once a pledge that all would live." |
| **Justice** | Fairness and moral rightness. Bruegel's peasants and Zhang Zeduan's festival scene show community and balance. |
| **Domesticity** | Life at home and daily routines. Vermeer, Chardin, and Rosler show the kitchen as a moral and political space. |
| **Ritual** | A repeated act with symbolic meaning. Meals in *Eat Drink Man Woman* and *The Last Supper* are rituals of connection. |
| **Materiality** | The physical quality of things. Texture and reflection in still life invite sensory attention and care. |
| **Hierarchy** | A system of rank or order. Who sits where at a table often reveals power and status. |
| **Globalization** | Links among nations through trade and culture. Dutch still lifes are early images of global exchange. |
| **Alienation** | Feeling of separation from people or meaning. Hogarth and Rosler show alienation in modern domestic life. |
| **Transformation** | Change of form or purpose. Cooking and art both transform raw material into meaning. |
| **Attention** | Focused seeing or awareness. For Berger, to see carefully is to act ethically. Vermeer's light teaches this. |
| **Reciprocity** | Mutual giving and receiving. A just meal involves exchange rather than possession. |
| **Exploitation** | Unfair use of people or resources. Imported goods in still lifes hint at unseen labor and colonial systems. |
| **Devotion** | Deep focus or faith. The Milkmaid's patient work becomes an image of quiet devotion. |
| **Waste** | Loss through neglect or excess. Rotten fruit and empty plates in still lifes warn against greed. |
