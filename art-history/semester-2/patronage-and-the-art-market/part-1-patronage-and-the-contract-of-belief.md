# Part 1: Patronage and the Contract of Belief

## Central Idea

Patronage is a contract that shapes meaning, subject matter, and how viewers are trained to see. Before the modern market, artworks were commissioned to serve public functions: devotion, civic identity, education, and legitimacy. This week builds the vocabulary for how commissioners and institutions control imagery through program, iconography, and site.

> "A fifteenth-century painting is the deposit of a social relationship. On one side there was a painter who made the picture, or at least supervised its making. On the other side there was somebody else who asked him to make it, provided funds for him to make it and, after he had made it, reckoned on using it in some way or other."
> — Michael Baxandall, *Painting and Experience in Fifteenth-Century Italy*

The word Baxandall reaches for is not patron but *client*: "an active, determining and not necessarily benevolent agent in the transaction of which the painting is the result." The painting is not a gift. It is a manufacture made to order, and the order tells us everything.

---

## Goals and Objectives

Establish patronage as a system of power, not simple sponsorship. Build a clear framework for analyzing who controls meaning in commissioned art.

**By the end of the week, students should be able to:**
- Define patronage, commission, iconography, program, site, and audience.
- Identify the stakeholders in a commission and describe their interests.
- Explain how viewing conditions and placement shape interpretation.
- Produce a diagram that links funding to narrative control.

## Reading

Michael Baxandall, *Painting and Experience in Fifteenth-Century Italy*
Read the sections on patron expectations and commissioning (Conditions of Trade, Ch. I, sections 1–5).

**What to look for:** Baxandall's argument is that the economic conditions of the trade are *directly visible* in the paintings. When you understand what clients paid for and how — materials versus skill, lapis lazuli specified by the ounce, clauses demanding the master's hand on every figure — you start to see the contract embedded in the brushwork. Read for how money shapes form.

## Key Quotes

**On the bespoke nature of the trade:**
> "The better sort of fifteenth-century painting was made on a bespoke basis, the client asking for a manufacture after his own specifications. Ready-made pictures were limited to such things as run-of-the-mill Madonnas and marriage chests painted by the less sought after artists in slack periods."

**On materials as meaning:**
> "The exotic and dangerous character of ultramarine was a means of accent that we, for whom dark blue is probably no more striking than scarlet or vermilion, are liable to miss."

**On the shift from material to skill:**
> "As the conspicuous consumption of gold and ultramarine became less important in the contracts, its place was filled by references to an equally conspicuous consumption of something else — skill."

**On the period eye:**
> "The beholder must use on the painting such visual skills as he has, very few of which are normally special to painting, and he is likely to use those skills his society esteems highly."

**On the function of religious images (John of Genoa's *Catholicon*, late 13th c.):**
> "Know that there were three reasons for the institution of images in churches. First, for the instruction of simple people... Second, so that the mystery of the incarnation and the examples of the Saints may be the more active in our memory... Third, to excite feelings of devotion, these being aroused more effectively by things seen than by things heard."

## Object Focus — *The Ambassadors*, Hans Holbein the Younger, 1533

**Oil on oak, 207 × 209.5 cm. National Gallery, London.**

Two men stand flanking a two-tiered shelf packed with objects: celestial and terrestrial globes, a lute, a case of flutes, arithmetic books, a sundial, a hymn book. In the foreground, an anamorphic skull cuts across the lower third of the composition at a diagonal — only readable from a sharp angle to the right of the painting.

**Key facts:**
- The figures are Jean de Dinteville, French ambassador to England (left), and Georges de Selve, Bishop of Lavaur (right). Both were in London in 1533 during Henry VIII's break with Rome.
- The objects on the shelf are organized by the medieval division of knowledge: the upper shelf holds astronomical instruments (celestial); the lower holds instruments of earthly learning (terrestrial). This is a program, not a still life.
- The lute has a broken string. The open hymn book shows Lutheran and Catholic versions of hymns side by side.
- The skull is only legible from an extreme oblique angle — traditionally the angle of someone entering or exiting the room to the left.
- The curtain in the upper left partially conceals a crucifix.

**Questions:**
1. Dinteville commissioned this painting. What did he need it to do for him?
2. Who is the intended audience, and what would they have recognized that we now need footnotes to see?
3. The skull is a *memento mori*. What does it mean that it can only be read from outside the frame of the image?
4. Berger writes that the function of portrait painting was to "underwrite and idealise a chosen social role." Does *The Ambassadors* do this?
5. What is the painting arguing about knowledge, power, and mortality — and who benefits from that argument?

## Key Terms

**Patronage** — A system in which a person, institution, or state funds the production of an artwork in exchange for control over its subject, program, or public function.

**Commission** — The specific agreement, explicit or implicit, between patron and artist that determines what the work will do, show, and mean.

**Iconography** — The system of symbols, figures, and references that carry meaning within a visual tradition.

**Program** — The organized sequence of images or themes within a larger work or space, designed to produce a specific interpretive experience.

**Site** — The physical location and conditions under which a work is displayed.

**Audience** — Not a neutral crowd, but a specific group with specific knowledge, social position, and relationship to power.

## Discussion Questions

- If Holbein was hired by Dinteville, whose interests does the painting serve? Can a single work serve more than one master at once?
- The shelf full of instruments looks like a celebration of human knowledge. The skull undermines it. Which one is the real argument of the painting?
- What would it mean to read this painting *against* its patron's intentions?
