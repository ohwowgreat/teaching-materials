# Part 2: The Design of Attention

## Central Idea

In the Renaissance and Counter-Reformation, the Catholic Church uses art to manage doctrine, public emotion, and narrative control. The Church's patronage is a system of image governance. It funds art while controlling iconography, legibility, and affect. Style becomes persuasion. Narrative clarity becomes a tool of authority.

> "Most fifteenth-century pictures are religious pictures... 'religious pictures' refers to more than just a certain range of subject matter; it means that the pictures existed to meet institutional ends, to help with specific intellectual and spiritual activities."
> — Michael Baxandall

## Reading

Francis Haskell, *Patrons and Painters*
Read a chapter section on Italian Baroque patronage networks, focusing on how patrons and institutions shaped subjects and public meaning.

**What to look for:** Haskell shows the network behind a commission — the chain of obligation, taste, and politics that runs from the cardinal's drawing room to the chapel wall. Where Baxandall focuses on the contract as a document, Haskell focuses on the relationship as a social system.

## Object Focus — *Calling of Saint Matthew*, Caravaggio, 1599–1600

**Oil on canvas, 322 × 340 cm. Contarelli Chapel, San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome.**

This painting was commissioned for a specific wall, in a specific room, to be seen by specific people arriving from a specific direction. That context is part of the work.

**Key facts:**
- The commission came from Cardinal Francesco Maria Del Monte, one of the most politically connected patrons in Rome.
- The scene depicts the moment Christ calls Matthew, a tax collector, to become a disciple.
- Caravaggio sets the scene in what looks like a contemporary Roman tavern, not a biblical landscape. The figures are dressed in 16th-century clothing. The only supernatural element is the diagonal shaft of light entering from the right.
- Christ's gesture is taken directly from Michelangelo's *Creation of Adam* on the Sistine ceiling. This is a citation, not an accident.
- The Counter-Reformation Council of Trent had decreed that religious art must be clear, emotionally legible, and doctrinally correct.

**Questions:**
1. The scene looks like it is happening in a tavern right now, not 2,000 years ago. What does that contemporaneity do to the viewer's relationship to the event being depicted?
2. Who commissioned this work, and what did they stand to gain from it?
3. The light does not come from a natural source in the room. It enters with Christ. What belief is that formal choice asking you to accept?
4. Caravaggio cites Michelangelo's gesture from the Sistine ceiling. Why?
5. The Council of Trent wanted art to be accessible and emotionally direct. Is this painting obedient to that directive, or does it do something more complicated?

## Key Quotes

**Fra Michele da Carcano (1492), cited in Baxandall, on the three functions of religious images:**
> "Images of the Virgin and the Saints were introduced for three reasons. First, on account of the ignorance of simple people... Second, images were introduced on account of our emotional sluggishness... Third, they were introduced on account of our unreliable memories."

**Baxandall on the painter as visualizer:**
> "The painter was a professional visualizer of the holy stories... The public mind was not a blank tablet on which the painters' representations of a story or person could impress themselves; it was an active institution of interior visualization with which every painter had to get along."

## The Counter-Reformation and Image Governance

The Council of Trent (1545–1563) issued explicit directives about the use of images in churches:
- Images must be legible to the uneducated. Obscurity is a failure.
- Images must move the viewer emotionally toward devotion and obedience.
- Images must be doctrinally accurate. Any ambiguity is a liability.
- The local bishop has authority to approve or reject images.

This is not background context. It is the commissioning brief.

---

## Key Terms

**Affect** — The emotional response a work is designed to produce in the viewer.

**Legibility** — The degree to which a work can be read and understood without specialized knowledge. High legibility is a political choice as much as an aesthetic one.

**Program** — The organized sequence of images or themes within a larger work or space.

**Institutional patronage** — Funding and control of art by a body rather than an individual.

**Citation** — The deliberate visual reference to an earlier work. In art made for educated audiences, citation is an argument.

## Discussion Questions

- The Council of Trent wanted art to be clear and emotionally direct. Is clarity neutral? Who benefits from an image that does not require interpretation?
- Caravaggio puts a biblical scene in a contemporary tavern. The Church accepted it. What does that tell us about what the Counter-Reformation actually wanted from realism?
- If you were designing a propaganda campaign for a powerful institution today, which of Caravaggio's formal strategies would you use, and why?
