# Part 4: Galleries and the Primary Market

## Overview

Galleries shape careers by controlling visibility, pricing, placement, and the story a body of work tells. The primary market is where living artists are first priced and positioned. Galleries establish coherence, manage scarcity, and translate practice into a legible public narrative. Prices function as signals inside a network.

**Duration:** 5 classes (1 week)

---

## Goals

By the end of this week, students should understand what galleries actually do beyond the transactional — placement, narrative construction, and long-term career shaping. They should learn how pricing in the primary market works as a symbolic system, not just an economic one, and develop the ability to read and construct value narratives around a body of work.

**By the end of the week, students should be able to:**
- Explain what galleries do beyond "selling," including placement and narrative construction
- Distinguish a coherent body of work from a loose set of pieces
- Describe how pricing functions symbolically in the primary market
- Compare gallery-backed and self-organized career models with specific examples
- Identify what is being traded in alternative distribution systems, not just what is being gained
- Assemble a basic pitch package using clear, concrete language

---

## Reading

Sarah Thornton, *7 Days in the Art World* — Chapter 1, "The Auction"

Thornton embeds herself inside Christie's as they prepare and execute a major evening sale. Read it as an ethnography of how value gets performed — the role of the specialist, the catalogue text, the guarantee, the atmosphere of the room. Annotate for: how price is constructed before the hammer falls, who the key actors are, and any moment where Thornton reveals something the participants would not say directly.

## Selected Works

- **Issy Wood, paintings (2019–present)** — Sadie Coles HQ. Central case study for coherence as a market condition.
- **Oscar Murillo, early paintings (2011–2014)** — David Zwirner. Gallery-managed scarcity and what happens when the secondary market moves faster than the gallery's placement strategy.
- **Damien Hirst, *The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living* (1991)** — White Cube model, scarcity through spectacle and scale.
- **Louise Bourgeois, *Maman* (1999)** — Cheim & Read; late-career retrospective framing.
- **Brad Troemel / UV Production House (2013–present)** — The e-commerce store as distribution and critique.
- **Wolfgang Tillmans, artist books and publications (1990s–present)** — The print circuit as primary distribution. Between Bridges as an alternative institutional model.

## Formative Assessment: Gallery Pitch Package v1

**Components:**
- 8–10 work samples (images or equivalent)
- Captions for each (title, date, medium, dimensions)
- Bio (120 words)
- Artist statement (200 words)
- Pitch email (clear subject line, short context, one specific ask)

**Assessed on:** Coherence of the body of work presented, clarity and positioning of the statement, professionalism of the bio, specificity of the pitch email, and evidence that the student has made a deliberate choice about who this package is addressed to. Not assessed on the quality of the artwork itself.

## Connections to Prior Units

Part 4 extends the symbolic capital vocabulary from Part 1 (Patronage and the Contract of Belief) into a contemporary register. The Baroque patron's role in controlling imagery and establishing legibility maps directly onto the gallery's role in constructing a coherent market identity. The gallery is a secular patronage system with a different accountability structure but the same fundamental logic.
